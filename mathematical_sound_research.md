# Mathematical Keyboard Sound Generation (DSP) Research

To mathematically generate the sound of a mechanical keyboard from scratch (Procedural Audio) rather than using pre-recorded `.ogg` files, we must break down the acoustic properties of keyboard switches and map them to Digital Signal Processing (DSP) algorithms.

## 1. The Anatomy of a Keystroke
A keystroke is not a single sound, but a sequence of acoustic events:
1. **Friction/Pre-travel (The Glide):** The stem rubbing against the housing. 
2. **The Actuation (Tactile/Click):** For tactile/clicky switches, a metal leaf springs back or a click-jacket drops.
3. **The Bottom-Out (The Impact):** The plastic stem violently strikes the bottom housing. This generates the loudest transient.
4. **The Resonance (The Case):** The vibrations travel through the switch plate, PCB, and keyboard case, which acts as an acoustic chamber.
5. **The Top-Out (The Return):** The spring pushes the stem back up, striking the top housing.

## 2. Switch Types & Their Signatures
* **Linear (Red, Black, Cream):** Pure impact. No mid-travel click. Sound is dominated by the bottom-out and top-out.
* **Tactile (Brown, Holy Panda):** Has a rounded tactile bump. Introduces a slight low-frequency "thud" right before the bottom-out.
* **Clicky (Blue, Box Jade):** Contains a secondary high-frequency, extremely sharp transient spike caused by a mechanical click-bar or jacket.

## 3. Mathematical Generation (How to synthesize it)
Instead of audio files, we use pure math to generate waveforms in real-time.

### A. The Impact (Bottom-out & Top-out)
* **Math:** Sine or Triangle wave oscillators with a very rapid ADSR (Attack, Decay, Sustain, Release) volume envelope.
* **Implementation:** A sharp attack (1-2ms), zero sustain, and a fast exponential decay (20-50ms). 

### B. The Friction (Scratchiness)
* **Math:** White or Pink Noise generator passed through a Bandpass filter.
* **Implementation:** Modulate the volume of the noise based on the keystroke speed.

### C. The Case Acoustics (Resonance)
* **Math:** Comb Filters, Delay Lines (Karplus-Strong string synthesis), or Convolution Reverb.
* **Implementation:** Pass the Impact and Friction sounds through a waveguide or resonator. A large, empty plastic case has a long decay and lower resonant frequency. A solid aluminum case has high damping (short decay) and higher resonant frequency.

## 4. Translating Physical Keyboard Mods to Mathematical Settings
To give users the ultimate "Custom Sound Maker", we can offer these interactive sliders which map directly to DSP algorithms:

| Keyboard Mod / Term | Physical Meaning | DSP / Mathematical Equivalent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

## Summary
By combining a **Noise Generator** (for friction) and a **Transient Oscillator** (for impact), routing them through **Envelopes** (ADSR), and passing the final signal through **Filters (EQ) and Reverb**, you can mathematically generate any mechanical keyboard sound in existence without a single `.ogg` file.



Advanced Procedural Audio Synthesis for Mechanical Keyboard AcousticsThe Paradigm of Procedural Audio in Acoustic SimulationThe simulation of mechanical keyboard acoustics represents a highly complex intersection of physical modeling, digital signal processing (DSP), and psychoacoustics. As typing on mechanical keyboards has evolved from a utilitarian necessity into a highly customized, sensory experience, the demand for precise acoustic feedback has surged. For digital applications seeking to emulate this experience mathematically—without the hardware constraints of mechanical switches—procedural audio offers a robust, highly customizable framework.Procedural audio is defined as sound generated as a process rather than stored as data. Traditional game and application audio design relies heavily on sample-based approaches, which involve the playback of pre-recorded audio files. While sample-based audio can capture high-fidelity moments in time, it suffers from severe limitations in interactive environments. The primary constraint is the "machine-gun effect," a psychoacoustic phenomenon where the rapid, repeated playback of a static audio sample shatters the illusion of realism, alerting the human auditory system to the artificiality of the sound. To combat this, sound designers often record dozens of variations of a single keystroke and apply real-time randomization to pitch and amplitude, which exponentially increases the application's memory footprint.Procedural sound synthesis circumvents these limitations by utilizing programmatic rules and algorithms to generate audio directly at runtime. As a result, procedural audio synthesizers require virtually zero storage space for audio assets, drastically reducing the application's memory overhead. More importantly, procedural models are inherently dynamic. Because the sound is calculated on a sample-by-sample basis (often at a rate of 44,100 or 48,000 times per second), the synthesizer can generate infinite, non-repetitive micro-variations based on real-time inputs such as simulated strike velocity, key position, and user-defined mechanical properties. This parametric flexibility is the cornerstone of a fully customizable keyboard simulation application, granting end-users the ability to manipulate mathematical variables to craft their own bespoke auditory experience without relying on a predefined library of sound files.The Mechanical Exciter: Switch Typologies and Acoustic ProfilesTo mathematically synthesize the sound of a keyboard, one must first decompose the physical instrument into its constituent acoustic components: the exciter and the resonator. Every keystroke is an impact event. The primary exciter in a mechanical keyboard is the switch mechanism itself, which dictates the initial transient shape, frequency spectrum, and amplitude of the acoustic wave before it propagates through the rest of the keyboard's body.Mechanical switches are broadly categorized into three typologies based on their tactile and acoustic behavior: linear, tactile, and clicky. Each typology requires a distinct mathematical excitation model to simulate accurately.Linear switches, such as the Cherry MX Red, Gateron Yellow, and various "silver" speed switches, feature a smooth vertical travel path with no physical bump or integrated click mechanism. The sound signature of a linear switch is almost entirely dominated by the impact of the internal plastic stem striking the bottom housing (the bottom-out) and the subsequent impact of the stem striking the upper housing upon the spring's release (the top-out). Because linear switches do not have mechanical impediments in their travel path, users often strike them with unencumbered force. However, switches with longer travel distances (such as the 4.0 mm travel of a Gateron Yellow compared to the 3.6 mm of a Kailh Pro) spread the kinetic energy of the impact over a slightly longer duration. This temporal spreading lowers the peak amplitude of the transient and shifts the spectral energy downward, emphasizing low-mid frequencies between 100 Hz and 800 Hz.Tactile switches, such as the Cherry MX Brown or the highly aggressive Boba U4T, introduce a physical bump on the internal contact leaf. This bump serves as a physical indicator of actuation, occurring before the switch bottoms out. Acoustically, the tactile bump generates a muted, secondary frequency transient prior to the primary impact. Furthermore, the physical resistance of the tactile bump reduces the terminal velocity of the descending stem. Data indicates that a tactile bump reduces the bottom-out force by approximately 15% to 20% compared to linear switches possessing similar spring weights. This reduction in terminal velocity translates mathematically to a less aggressive stem collision, resulting in an inherently lower peak amplitude and a rounder acoustic decay. Consequently, tactile switches like the MX Brown often produce a quieter overall acoustic profile (measuring around 52-55 dB at close range) compared to their linear counterparts, provided all other housing variables remain constant.Clicky switches are engineered specifically for audible confirmation and represent the loudest and most spectrally distinct category. These switches integrate independent mechanical components that snap or drop during the downstroke to generate a sharp, high-frequency transient. There are two primary click mechanisms: the click-jacket and the click-bar. The Cherry MX Blue and Gateron Blue utilize a click-jacket, a plastic collar that drops rapidly at the actuation point. This produces a high-pitched, slightly rattly click peaking between 3.2 kHz and 3.8 kHz, outputting an average of 65-68 dB. The Kailh Box White, Box Jade, and Box Navy utilize a click-bar, which is a flexible metal wire that is pulled back and released by the descending stem. The click-bar produces a much sharper, crisper, and louder snap, often reaching 70-72 dB in the case of the thicker bar found in the Box Jade. In both designs, the click mechanism actuates approximately 1 to 2 milliseconds before the stem bottoms out against the housing. Polycarbonate housings, common in traditional Cherry switches, transmit high-frequency kinetic energy highly efficiently, resulting in a bright, snappy sound profile. Polybutylene terephthalate (PBT) and Polyoxymethylene (POM) housings add further mass and internal damping, significantly reducing high-end ringing. To achieve a highly authentic simulation, the mathematical model must treat the switch housing as a dedicated low-pass filter applied directly to the excitation signal before the sound waves propagate into the keyboard chassis.Acoustic Cavities and Resonators: Plates, Cases, and KeycapsOnce the switch generates the initial impact vibration, the acoustic energy transfers into the surrounding resonating bodies: the keycap mounted above the switch, the plate securing the switch, and the external chassis encapsulating the assembly. Each of these physical components possesses unique densities, acoustic impedances, and resonant frequencies that aggressively shape the final sound wave.The keycap acts as the primary acoustic chamber and is the first point of contact for the user. The material and thickness of the keycap dictate how vibrations are either absorbed or amplified. Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) is a thin, smooth plastic commonly used in mass-produced keyboards. ABS vibrates freely, amplifying higher frequencies and producing a brighter, higher-pitched clarity. In contrast, Polybutylene terephthalate (PBT) is a thicker, denser polymer. The increased mass of PBT acts as a mechanical dampener, swallowing high-frequency vibrations rather than broadcasting them. When an internal switch stem impacts a PBT keycap, the material heavily dampens the transient, shifting the fundamental resonance toward a deeper, lower-pitched tone. Metal keycaps represent the extreme end of this spectrum; their dense mass absorbs nearly all subtle high-frequency friction from the switch travel, muting the natural clack and producing an incredibly deep, resonant bottom-out. The profile of the keycap (e.g., the tall SA profile versus the low Cherry profile) also dictates the volume of air trapped beneath the plastic, acting as a Helmholtz resonator that amplifies specific narrow frequency bands.The mounting plate, which holds the switches in place above the printed circuit board (PCB), dictates the sustain and ringing of the sound. Rigid metal plates made of steel or brass possess very low internal damping. They transmit kinetic vibrations highly efficiently across the entire keyboard array, producing a louder, brighter sound characterized by prolonged, high-frequency ringing. Conversely, plates manufactured from softer, more flexible materials such as Polycarbonate (PC), Polyoxymethylene (POM), or FR4 (woven glass reinforced epoxy resin) absorb significant kinetic vibration. These softer plates heavily dampen the sound, cutting the sustain of the high frequencies and resulting in a quieter, deeper acoustic profile.The external keyboard chassis functions as a macro-resonant cavity, much like the body of an acoustic guitar. Thin plastic cases possess highly reflective internal surfaces that allow sound waves to bounce continuously, amplifying emptiness and creating a hollow, echoing sound. Solid aluminum cases, due to their immense mass, contain the kinetic energy tightly. The density of the metal prevents rogue vibrations, yielding a sharper, highly focused impact sound, though occasionally introducing a distinct metallic ping if not properly insulated. Furthermore, the method by which the plate is mounted to the case drastically alters vibration transfer. A rigid tray mount, where the PCB is screwed directly to the bottom of the case, forces a bright, resonant attack with a long high-frequency sustain. In contrast, a gasket mount isolates the plate between strips of compressible Poron foam or silicone. This isolation absorbs kinetic energy before it can reach the external case, drastically reducing high-end sustain and producing a warmer, more muted tone.Psychoacoustic Profiling: Thock, Clack, and CreamyIn the context of the enthusiast mechanical keyboard community, sounds are frequently described using subjective, onomatopoeic terminology. For a procedural synthesis application to be successful, it must accurately map these subjective descriptions—specifically "thock," "clack," and "creamy"—to objective digital signal processing parameters and spectral analyses.The term "thock" describes a deep, solid, low-frequency sound that is completely devoid of high-pitched rattle, buzz, or metallic ping. It implies heavy, dense materials that absorb all harsh transients, leaving a clean, singular thump followed rapidly by silence. Spectrographic analysis of a thocky keyboard reveals that the vast majority of the acoustic energy is concentrated tightly within the low-mid fundamental frequency bands, specifically between 100 Hz and 400 Hz. Achieving a true thock in a physical board often requires PBT keycaps, a flexible POM plate, and a gasket-mounted chassis. In a procedural synthesis environment, generating a thock profile requires setting the global high-pass filter cutoff exceedingly low, applying rapid exponential decay envelopes to all high-frequency resonant modes, and maximizing the amplitude of the lowest fundamental frequencies. Thock is not inherently loud; rather, it is characterized by its high-density acoustic mass and lack of high-frequency sustain.Conversely, "clack" describes a brighter, thinner, and highly percussive sound. If thock represents a heavy wooden block falling onto a velvet cushion, clack represents the striking of a smooth river stone against another hard surface. Clack is dominated by high-frequency transients, with the spectral energy peaking sharply between 2 kHz and 5 kHz. It is the hallmark of rigid assemblies: ABS keycaps striking against solid metal plates mounted rigidly into aluminum or hard plastic cases. A clacky profile implies functional efficiency and a lack of dampening materials. To mathematically synthesize a clack, the procedural engine must preserve higher-order resonant modes, reduce the damping coefficients applied to the 2 kHz to 5 kHz frequency bands, and utilize an excitation signal with an extremely fast, aggressive attack phase.The terms "creamy," "buttery," or "marbly" represent a synthesis profile that is overwhelmingly smooth, highly damped, and meticulously tuned to remove all internal friction. A creamy keyboard sounds thick and muted, possessing a rounded attack rather than a sharp impact. Physically, this profile is achieved through meticulous lubrication of the switch stems to eradicate the high-frequency scratching of plastic on plastic, combined with the extensive use of internal acoustic foams that fill the empty voids within the case. In the spectral domain, a creamy sound exhibits an incredibly fast decay on all upper harmonics, leaving a highly focused, warm mid-range resonance. Simulating a buttery sound mathematically requires aggressively muting the stochastic noise generators that represent friction, applying an extreme low-pass filter to the excitation pulse to round off the transient, and utilizing heavy, broadband damping across the entire modal network to prevent any frequency from ringing beyond the initial impact.Mathematical Foundations of Procedural Audio and Modal SynthesisTo mathematically generate these highly specific acoustic profiles, the application must abandon sample playback in favor of physically inspired sound synthesis, most notably Modal Synthesis. Modal synthesis is grounded in the physical reality that the complex vibrational behavior of any rigid object can be perfectly decomposed into a set of independent, single-degree-of-freedom oscillators, known as modes. Each mode oscillates at a single, distinct frequency and decays exponentially over time.The theoretical foundation of modal sound synthesis begins with the linear deformation equation for a three-dimensional linear elastic dynamics model. When an external force (such as a switch stem bottoming out) stimulates a rigid body (such as a keycap or keyboard plate), the resulting vibrations across the object's geometry can be expressed by the following differential equation:$$M\ddot{x} + C\dot{x} + Kx = f$$Where $M$ represents the mass matrix of the object, dictating its inertia. $C$ is the Rayleigh damping matrix, which determines how quickly kinetic energy is dissipated as heat. $K$ is the stiffness matrix, defining the object's resistance to deformation. The vector $x$ represents the nodal displacements across the object, while $\ddot{x}$ and $\dot{x}$ represent the acceleration and velocity of those nodes, respectively. Finally, $f$ represents the external excitation force vector applied to the object.Calculating this coupled equation in real-time for an object with millions of microscopic nodes is computationally impossible. Therefore, the system undergoes a process known as generalized eigenvalue decomposition. By solving for the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the mass and stiffness matrices, the highly complex, coupled physical system is mathematically decoupled into a vast array of independent equations.The resulting decoupled system takes the following form:$$\ddot{q} + (\alpha I + \beta \Lambda)\dot{q} + \Lambda q = U^T f$$Where $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues (which directly relate to the resonant frequencies of the object), and $q$ satisfies the spatial transformation $x = Uq$.The analytical solution to this decoupled equation is a parallel bank of damped sinusoidal waves. Each independent wave represents a distinct mode of the object's vibration. The mathematical expression for the $i$-th mode in the time domain is:$$q_i(t) = a_i e^{-c_i t} \sin(2\pi \omega_i t + \theta_i)$$Where $\omega_i$ is the resonant angular frequency of the specific mode. The variable $c_i$ represents the damping coefficient, which governs the exponential rate at which the sine wave decays to zero. The scalar $a_i$ is the initial amplitude, which is excited based on the magnitude of the impact force and the spatial location of the strike. Finally, $\theta_i$ represents the initial phase of the wave.A comprehensive modal synthesis engine represents the complete acoustic character of a keyboard component as a collection of $N$ modes. Each mode is fully characterized by a simple tuple of three parameters: frequency, amplitude, and loss factor (decay rate). The final synthesized sound output to the user is achieved simply by summing the instantaneous outputs of all $N$ modes in real-time.Inharmonicity and Material DensityIf one were modeling a perfectly ideal string clamped at both ends, the wave equation would dictate that the modal frequencies are exact integer harmonics of the fundamental frequency ($f_k \propto k$). However, the components of a mechanical keyboard—thick plastic keycaps, irregular metal plates, and rigid chassis—do not behave like ideal strings. Their complex physical geometries and rigid material densities result in highly inharmonic resonant modes.To accurately simulate the sound of solid objects rather than musical strings, the procedural model must stretch the modal frequencies mathematically. This is achieved by introducing an inharmonicity factor, denoted as $B$. The fast approximation for a stiff, rigid body modifies the modal frequency distribution according to the following equation:$$f_k \propto k \sqrt{1 + B k^2}$$When $B$ is set to a small positive value (typically on the order of $10^{-3}$), the higher-order modes are stretched upward, deviating from perfect harmonicity. This stretching introduces the slightly metallic, dense, and non-tonal qualities characteristic of rigid plastic and metal impacts. By adjusting the parameter $B$, the simulation can seamlessly transition between materials; increasing $B$ simulates a highly stiff, dense metal plate, while decreasing it mimics a softer, more uniform plastic. Furthermore, by exposing the fundamental frequency variable ($f_0$) to the user interface, the application can mathematically pitch-shift the entire keyboard sound up or down, preserving the inharmonic ratios while allowing the user to tune the "depth" of the keystroke.Digital Filter Implementation and the Biquad NetworkWhile the damped sinusoidal equation is mathematically pure, computing sine and exponential functions for dozens of simultaneous modes per keystroke at an audio rate of 48,000 samples per second is computationally demanding. In a digital signal processing environment, modal synthesis is most efficiently implemented not by calculating sine waves directly, but by utilizing recursive digital filters. Specifically, the modes are modeled using a parallel bank of second-order Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) filters, commonly known as biquad filters.A biquad filter is a flexible, two-pole, two-zero digital filter whose coefficients can be explicitly calculated to resonate at a specific frequency with a specific bandwidth (or Q-factor). By exciting a parallel array of biquad filters with an input signal, the filters will "ring" at their tuned frequencies, perfectly mimicking the behavior of physical resonant modes.The behavior of a modeled signal utilizing a bank of $M$ modal biquad filters is expressed in the Z-domain by its transfer function:$$\hat{H}(z) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{b_{0,m} + b_{1,m}z^{-1} + b_{2,m}z^{-2}}{1 + a_{1,m}z^{-1} + a_{2,m}z^{-2}}$$Where $z^{-1}$ and $z^{-2}$ represent one-sample and two-sample delays, respectively. The feed-forward coefficients ($b_{0}, b_{1}, b_{2}$) and the feedback coefficients ($a_{1}, a_{2}$) for each mode $m$ are mathematically derived from the target resonant frequency $\omega_m$ and the damping factor $c_m$.This recursive biquad architecture provides immense flexibility. To alter the simulated material of the keyboard in real-time, the application simply updates the five filter coefficients for each mode. If a user slides a UI control from a "Clacky ABS Keycap" setting to a "Thocky PBT Keycap" setting, the DSP engine instantaneously recalculates the coefficients to lower the cutoff frequencies, increase the Q-factor of the fundamental mode, and heavily increase the damping (altering the $a_1, a_2$ coefficients) on the high-frequency modes. This dynamic filter network is what allows for the "infinite customization" requested, as millions of coefficient permutations can be generated programmatically without loading a single new audio file.The Excitation Signal: Raised-Cosine Pulses and Stochastic FrictionA filter network, no matter how perfectly tuned, is silent until it receives kinetic energy. In modal synthesis, this energy is provided by an excitation signal, which is fed into the input of the parallel biquad array. The excitation signal represents the physical impact of the switch stem colliding with the housing.Modeling the Physical ImpactA naive approach to triggering the filters would be to use a Dirac delta function (a digital impulse containing a single sample at maximum amplitude). However, a perfect impulse contains equal energy across all frequencies and represents an infinitely fast impact. In physical reality, collisions between plastics possess a finite duration, meaning the impact force rises and falls over a microscopic fraction of a second.To mathematically approximate the true force of a physical collision, procedural synthesizers often employ a shaped pulse, most notably the Raised-Cosine or Hann pulse. In digital communications, raised-cosine filters are utilized for pulse-shaping to minimize intersymbol interference, characterized by a smooth, band-limited frequency spectrum that minimizes high-frequency rippling. In the context of physical modeling, the time-domain representation of a raised-cosine pulse perfectly emulates the rounded, finite acceleration and deceleration of a physical object striking another.The time-domain force of the impact excitation can be mathematically expressed as:$$f_e(t) = f_{e,\text{max}} \sin^2\left(\frac{\zeta\pi(t - t_e)}{2T_e}\right)$$Where $t_e$ is the start time of the impact, $T_e$ is the total duration of the collision, and $f_{e,\text{max}}$ is the maximum amplitude of the force. The variable $\zeta$ is a scaling factor used to differentiate between a strike ($\zeta = 2$) and a pluck ($\zeta = 1$).By altering the duration parameter $T_e$, the application can simulate the material hardness of the switch stem. A very short $T_e$ produces a wide band of high-frequency energy, triggering the upper resonant modes and simulating the sharp "clack" of rigid polycarbonate plastics. Conversely, a longer $T_e$ acts as an inherent low-pass filter on the excitation signal, smoothly rolling off high frequencies before they even reach the resonator, thereby perfectly simulating the softer "thud" of dense nylon or heavily damped stems.Excitation Matrices for Switch TypologiesTo provide full customization across the various switch types, the excitation signal must be structurally modified based on the user's selection:Linear Switch: The simplest excitation model. A single raised-cosine pulse is generated at the exact moment of the bottom-out. The amplitude of the pulse ($f_{e,\text{max}}$) is scaled dynamically based on the user's typing velocity.Tactile Switch: A two-stage sequential excitation model. First, a low-amplitude, heavily low-pass-filtered pulse is generated to simulate the soft impact of the metal leaf against the tactile bump. This is followed closely by the primary bottom-out pulse. The duration between these two pulses must be tightly calibrated to simulate the physical distance of the tactile event.Clicky Switch (Click-Jacket/Click-Bar): A complex, multi-stage excitation sequence. To simulate a click-bar, a sharp, high-frequency burst of energy—often modeled as a rapidly decaying, high-pitch exponentially damped sinusoid—is injected into the signal 1 to 2 milliseconds prior to a secondary, wider raised-cosine pulse that represents the physical stem bottom-out.Simulating Scratchiness, Friction, and LubricationAn essential aspect of modern custom mechanical keyboards is their smoothness, which enthusiasts achieve by painstakingly disassembling switches to apply synthetic lubricants (such as Krytox 205g0) to the internal slider rails. Unlubricated, "stock" switches exhibit a distinct acoustic profile characterized as "scratchy". This scratchiness is the acoustic byproduct of continuous, high-frequency micro-collisions caused by the friction between two unpolished plastic surfaces sliding against one another.In a procedural synthesis environment, continuous friction is mathematically modeled using stochastic (random) noise generators. To simulate scratchiness, a continuous white noise source is passed through an adjustable band-pass filter. The center frequency and the amplitude of this filtered noise are dynamically mapped to the simulated velocity of the descending keypress. When the user strikes a key, this noise generator triggers during the travel phase, injecting high-frequency static into the mix just prior to the primary impact pulse.To simulate the application of lubrication (the highly desired "creamy" or "buttery" settings), the user interface allows the individual to adjust a "Lube Amount" slider. Mathematically, increasing this slider performs three simultaneous operations: it drives the amplitude gain of the stochastic friction noise generator down to zero, eliminating the scratchiness; it applies a steep low-pass filter to the primary raised-cosine excitation pulse, removing the highest-frequency attack transients; and it slightly lowers the center frequencies of the modal resonators to mimic the added mass of the viscous fluid.Simulating Physical Modifications: The Delany-Bazley Model and Acoustic DampingOne of the most frequent requests in the mechanical keyboard community is to achieve a "thocky" or "creamy" sound by installing acoustic dampeners. Users routinely pack their keyboard chassis with specialized materials like Poron foam, PE foam, or even multiple layers of masking tape applied to the back of the PCB (colloquially known as the "Tempest Mod" or "butter paper"). These modifications serve a specific physical purpose: they alter the acoustic impedance of the empty air cavities within the case, absorbing high-frequency reflections and allowing only the low-frequency fundamentals to escape.To simulate the insertion of "butter paper" or acoustic foam procedurally, the digital signal processing chain must incorporate mathematical models of sound absorption in porous materials. The industry standard for predicting the acoustic behavior of such materials is the Delany-Bazley model. The Delany-Bazley model is an empirical set of equations that calculates the complex characteristic acoustic impedance and the complex wave number of a porous material based primarily on a single physical macroscopic parameter: the material's specific airflow resistivity.When a user enables the "Butter Paper" or "Case Foam" setting in the application, the procedural engine routes the summed output of the modal biquad network through an additional digital filter designed to mimic the transfer function derived from the Delany-Bazley equations. In practice, inserting dense PE foam or tape acts as an aggressive acoustic low-pass filter and broad-spectrum dampener. The Delany-Bazley filter mathematically reduces the Q-factor (bandwidth resonance) of the highest-frequency biquads, preventing them from sustaining. It preserves the fundamental modal frequency $f_0$ (the 100 Hz to 400 Hz "thock" range) while almost completely attenuating any modal frequencies $f_k$ where the index $k > 3$.This sophisticated algorithmic absorption perfectly mirrors the physical reality of the modification. The masking tape or PE foam reflects the dense, low-frequency sound waves back toward the user while trapping and scattering the harsh, high-frequency transients within the porous material. The result is the coveted "marbly," "buttery," or "creamy" acoustic signature, achieved entirely through the rigorous application of empirical acoustic formulas.Software Architecture: Real-Time DSP in the Web Audio APIFor a typing simulation application to be convincing, the acoustic feedback must synchronize flawlessly with the user's physical key presses. Human perception is incredibly sensitive to audio-visual and tactile-audio latency. If the time delta between the user's finger striking their physical membrane keyboard and the simulated mechanical "thock" emitting from the speakers exceeds roughly 15 to 30 milliseconds, the illusion of physical interaction is shattered, and the application feels unresponsive and disjointed.Overcoming Main Thread Latency with AudioWorkletIf the application is built as a web application or deployed as a cross-platform desktop application using frameworks like Electron, traditional JavaScript processing is woefully inadequate for real-time procedural synthesis. Early iterations of the Web Audio API relied on the ScriptProcessorNode, which executed its audio callbacks directly on the browser's main UI thread. Because JavaScript is single-threaded, any layout rendering, DOM manipulation, or garbage collection occurring on the main thread would block the audio processing, resulting in catastrophic latency spikes and severe audio glitching (dropouts in the audio stream).Modern procedural synthesis necessitates the adoption of the AudioWorklet interface, a massive architectural shift in web audio processing. The AudioWorklet provides an execution context—the AudioWorkletGlobalScope—that runs strictly on a dedicated, high-priority audio rendering thread, entirely separate from the main JavaScript thread.By creating a subclass of the AudioWorkletProcessor, the mathematical generation of the modal synthesis, the calculation of the biquad filter coefficients, and the generation of the raised-cosine excitation signals all occur synchronously with the underlying audio hardware's relentless demand for sample frames (typically in blocks of 128 samples). This unblocks the audio engine from the UI, enabling the ultra-low latency required for interactive typing.High-Performance DSP with WebAssembly (WASM) and RustWhile the AudioWorklet solves the threading issue, JavaScript itself is not optimized for the intense, sample-by-sample mathematical operations required to run dozens of parallel biquad filters per keystroke. Attempting to compute complex matrices and trigonometry functions in JavaScript can lead to CPU clipping and thermal throttling on lower-end devices.To achieve the performance of native C++ audio plugins, the mathematical algorithms should be written in a high-performance, low-level systems programming language. Rust is heavily favored in the modern procedural audio community due to its strict memory safety guarantees and zero-cost abstractions. The entire DSP logic—including the state preservation of the biquad filters, the calculation of the Delany-Bazley transfer functions, and the stochastic noise generation—can be written in Rust and compiled directly into WebAssembly (WASM).The resulting highly optimized WASM binary is loaded directly into the AudioWorkletGlobalScope. When the application detects a physical keydown event via the browser's event listener, the main thread instantly dispatches a lightweight message via a MessagePort to the AudioWorklet. The Worklet instantly triggers the WASM DSP engine, which executes the exact mathematical formulas required, fills a Float32Array with the synthesized waveform data, and passes it directly to the BaseAudioContext.destination (the physical speakers). This architecture bypasses JavaScript entirely during the critical audio rendering phase, ensuring that the procedural keyboard responds with native, zero-latency precision.Advanced Real-Time Interactivity: Velocity and Top-Out DynamicsA final layer of acoustic realism is achieved by ensuring the synthesized sound reacts non-linearly to the user's input behavior. In basic sample-based engines, a user typing 120 words per minute triggers the exact same audio file repeatedly, leading to a synthetic, unnatural drone. A procedural model is aware of time and velocity.While standard membrane and laptop keyboards lack pressure-sensitive velocity tracking, the application can infer the user's typing intensity mathematically by calculating the delta time between consecutive keydown events. Rapid bursts of typing indicate high kinetic energy. The procedural engine maps this inferred typing speed directly to the impact force parameter ($f_{e,\text{max}}$) of the raised-cosine excitation pulse. Consequently, faster typing mathematically generates a louder, slightly brighter keystroke, exactly mimicking the physical reality of striking mechanical switches with greater force.Furthermore, the engine monitors keyup events to simulate the "top-out" acoustics of a mechanical switch. When a physical key is released, the internal spring forces the stem rapidly upward, striking the top housing and generating a distinct, secondary acoustic transient. The force of this top-out pulse is dependent on the simulated spring weight of the switch, which is an exposed parameter for the user. A heavier simulated spring (e.g., 60g) returns the stem with greater velocity, mathematically triggering a louder, sharper top-out "clack" upon the keyup event, whereas a lighter spring (e.g., 45g) generates a softer return sound.Comprehensive Parameterization Matrix for End-User CustomizationTo satisfy the core requirement of providing the user with "as many settings as possible" to facilitate "full customization," the mathematical architecture must abstract the highly complex DSP variables into an intuitive, user-facing matrix.
